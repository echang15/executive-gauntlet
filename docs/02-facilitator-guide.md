# FACILITATOR'S GUIDE: "The Friday Counterfeit"

**Format:** 80-minute live leadership simulation
**Participants:** 20 students in 4 teams of 5
**Role Player:** 1 person playing the Restaurant Group Owner
**Your job:** Keep time, hold the line on structure, and make sure the learning lands. Do not coach teams toward an answer.

## Learning Objectives

By the end, participants can:
1. Lead with the answer (BLUF) under time pressure.
2. Choose the least-bad option when every option has a cost (triage).
3. Defend a decision to a skeptical executive, with eye contact and without a script.
4. Communicate one decision to three different audiences (owner, kitchen, guests).
5. Name how this applies to real enterprise risk, incident response and technology leadership.

---

## Master Timeline

| Clock | Phase | Min | Your Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 00:00 – 15:00 | **Primer** | 15 | Teach BLUF, triage, pitch structure |
| 15:00 – 20:00 | **The Drop** | 5 | Form teams, distribute briefings |
| 20:00 – 45:00 | **War Room** | 25 | Timekeep; deliver inject at **35:00** |
| 45:00 – 70:00 | **The Gauntlet** | 25 | Run 4 pitches, about 6 min each |
| 70:00 – 80:00 | **Debrief** | 10 | Break character; run debrief questions |

## 1. Materials and Room Setup

### Before participants arrive (arrive 30 minutes early)

- **Four tables**, spaced so teams cannot overhear each other. Five chairs each. Label them **Table 1 to 4**.
- **One visual countdown timer**, visible from every table (projector, large display or screen share). Preload these countdowns: 15:00, 5:00, 25:00 and 3:00.
- **A "Pitch Stage"**: one table or two chairs at the front facing the room. The Role Player sits here, with space for one team's spokespeople to stand in front.
- **Printed materials**
  - 20 copies of the Participant Briefing (Document 1), face down at each seat.
  - 4 copies of the **Mid-Point Inject card** (script below), in sealed envelopes marked "OPEN AT 35:00." Keep them with you.
  - Blank paper and pens at each table, plus one whiteboard or flip chart per table if available.
- **Slide or whiteboard** with the pitch structure: Current State, Proposed Action, Immediate Cost, The Ask.
- **A bell, chime or hand-held timer** for pitch time signals.
- **Brief the Role Player** at least 15 minutes before the session. Confirm they have Document 3. Agree on one subtle signal to end an over-long Q&A.

### Seating
Assign teams in advance if you can. Mix backgrounds and majors so no table is all technical or all business.

## 2. The Executive Primer (00:00 – 15:00)

**Tone:** Calm, direct and a little urgent. You are modeling what you are about to teach.

### 00:00 – 02:00 | Frame the session
> "For the next 80 minutes you're not students. You're the operating team of a company in crisis. You will make a real decision with incomplete information and defend it to an executive who does not have time for you. Nobody gets a perfect answer. The skill we're training is deciding well anyway, and saying it clearly."

Preview the arc: briefing, 25-minute prep, a 3-minute pitch, then a debrief.

### 02:00 – 07:00 | BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)

**Core idea:** Put the decision and the conclusion in the first sentence. Context and reasoning follow only to support it.

**Talking points**
- Executives decide in seconds and read the first line of everything. If your recommendation is in sentence three, it may never be read.
- **Burying the lead feels polite but costs time.** Under pressure, it reads as indecision.
- **BLUF formula:** *Recommendation + Why (one reason) + What I need from you.*

**Demonstrate the contrast (do this live, 60 seconds):**

*Weak:* "So we've been looking at the supplier situation, and there are a lot of factors, like the inventory and the reservations, and there's the journalist, and we think maybe we should consider..."

*Strong:* "We recommend pulling the supplier product tonight and serving a clearly relabeled menu. It costs us about $40,000 and some revenue, but it protects the brand from a bigger loss. I need your approval to call the Chef in the next 10 minutes."

**Student-friendly analogy:** "It's the difference between a text that says *'Hey, got a sec? So I was thinking about the project and...'* and one that says *'Server is down, I'm rolling back, need your OK.'*"

**Quick drill (60 seconds):** Ask two volunteers to turn "We're out of time to finish the project and need to decide what to cut" into one BLUF sentence.

### 07:00 – 11:00 | Triage: choosing the least-bad option

**Core idea:** In a crisis there is no "good" option on the table. The skill is comparing bad options by **cost, reversibility and speed**.

**Talking points**
- Medical triage does not try to save everyone equally. It ranks by **what is most urgent and most recoverable**.
- **Three triage questions** (put on the board):
  1. **What can I recover from, and what can't I?** Money is usually recoverable. Trust often is not.
  2. **What do I know vs. what am I assuming?** Name your assumptions out loud.
  3. **What decision can I reverse, and which one is a one-way door?**
- **Doing nothing is a decision.** Delay has a cost, usually the most expensive one.
- **Warn them:** a strategy of "serve it and hope" or "cancel everything" is not automatically wrong, but it must be defended with numbers and consequences. *Never signal a preferred answer.*

### 11:00 – 14:00 | The Pitch: Current State, Proposed Action, Immediate Cost, The Ask

Walk through each element with a one-line example:

| Element | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Current State** | One or two sentences on the facts. | "The article drops at 5:00 PM and we hold $40K in affected stock." |
| **Proposed Action** | Your BLUF recommendation. | "We pull the product and serve a replacement menu tonight." |
| **Immediate Cost** | The honest price tag. | "About $40K plus roughly X in lost covers." |
| **The Ask** | The exact thing you need now. | "Authorize a call to the Chef and approve a comp policy." |

**Delivery rules to teach**
- **Don't read.** Notes are fine, a script is not. The Owner will interrupt a reader.
- **Eye contact.** Look at the person you're persuading.
- **When interrupted, answer the question first.** Then return to your structure. An interruption is a chance to show you know your plan, not a derailment.
- **Say the cost out loud.** Hiding it destroys credibility instantly.

### 14:00 – 15:00 | Set expectations
> "The Owner will push back hard. That is the exercise. If you get interrupted, that means they're engaged. Stay calm and keep it short."

Confirm questions, then move straight into The Drop.

---

## 3. The Drop (15:00 – 20:00)

- **15:00:** Announce the 4 teams of 5. Send people to their tables.
- **15:30:** Read aloud only: *"It's 2:00 PM on Friday. You have until 5:00 PM. Turn over your briefing."*
- **16:00 – 19:00:** Participants read silently. Do not answer strategy questions. Redirect with: *"That's one of the things you have to decide."* Clarifying logistics questions are fine.
- **19:00:** Remind them: 3 deliverables, a spokesperson (1 or 2), and a 3-minute pitch.
- **19:30:** Final reminder that the War Room starts in 30 seconds.
- **20:00:** Start the 25:00 timer. **Say: "War Room is open. Clock is running."**

---

## 4. The War Room (20:00 – 45:00)

### What you do
- Walk the room quietly. Observe. **Do not coach.**
- Note for the debrief: who is leading, who is silent, how teams argue, and what they decide.
- Time calls: **at 30:00** (10 min elapsed), **at 40:00** (5 min left), **at 43:00** (2 min left), **at 44:30** ("Spokespeople, stand up").
- If a team is stuck on analysis paralysis, ask only: *"What would you tell the Owner if the call were in 60 seconds?"*

### MID-POINT INJECT: exactly at 35:00 (15 minutes into the War Room)

**Preparation**
- Pre-seal four identical copies of the card below in envelopes. Each is marked "OPEN AT 35:00: URGENT."
- At **34:30**, stand at the center of the room holding all four envelopes so everyone sees you.

**Delivery script (say this, then walk the envelopes to tables)**
> *[Pause. Lower your voice. Walk quickly.]*
> "Stop. New information just came in. Everyone, hands off your pens for ten seconds."
>
> *[Hand an envelope to each table, one after another, without pausing.]*
>
> "This was just delivered. Read it as a team. The clock does not stop."

**Physical delivery**
- Don't announce the contents aloud. Each table reads it themselves, and some will react differently.
- Place the envelope **in front of the spokesperson, not the loudest voice.**
- Leave the timer running. Don't extend time.
- Return to the edge of the room and watch the reactions. The most useful debrief material of the day is how each team handles the first 60 seconds after reading it.

**Inject card text (print on distinct paper, e.g., red or bright yellow)**

> **URGENT: 3:05 PM**
> **From: Corporate Counsel**
>
> Our supplier has just responded to the journalist's inquiry. They **deny all claims** and have issued a statement through their attorney.
>
> They have also sent us a formal letter. They state that if the restaurant group **pulls, returns or publicly disparages** their product, they will:
> 1. File a **defamation lawsuit** against the group, and
> 2. Pursue **breach-of-contract penalties** under our exclusive supply agreement.
>
> The letter does not specify the penalty amount. Counsel says it "could be substantial."
>
> *You still have no independent proof that the journalist's claims are true or false. The article still publishes at 5:00 PM.*

**What the inject is designed to do**
- Make the "just pull it" option legally and financially riskier.
- Make the "serve it" option feel safer, which it isn't.
- Test whether teams update their plan or defend the old one.

**If asked questions about the inject:** answer in character, as Counsel or a staff member, and keep it short:
- *"Can we get proof?"* "Not in the next three hours."
- *"How big are the penalties?"* "Unknown. Possibly six figures."
- *"Can we say we're 'pausing' the product?"* "That's one way to describe it. You'd have to defend that wording."

Do not resolve the dilemma for them.

## 5. The Gauntlet (45:00 – 70:00)

### Setup
- Role Player seated at the Pitch Stage. Timer visible.
- Draw the order of pitching by lot, or choose by table number. Announce it.
- **Budget:** 3 minutes of pitch (interruptions count against the pitch time and extend into the rest of the slot) plus about 2.5 minutes of Q&A, then a 30-second transition. About 6 minutes per team, 4 teams, 24 minutes total, with 1 minute of buffer.

### Your role during each pitch
1. **Announce:** "Table [N], you have three minutes. Role Player, the floor is yours."
2. Start the 3:00 timer when the spokesperson begins speaking.
3. **Chime at 2:00 and 3:00.** At 3:00, the Role Player may continue to question for up to 2.5 more minutes.
4. **Hard stop:** At about 5:30 to 6:00 elapsed, thank the team and call the next table. If the Role Player is mid-question, let them finish the sentence, then move on.
5. Take notes using the scoring checklist below.

### Observation checklist (private)
For each team, note:
- [ ] BLUF in the first sentence?
- [ ] Stated the dollar cost?
- [ ] Addressed the supplier lawsuit threat?
- [ ] Named an owner and a next step for the Ask?
- [ ] Were the three deliverables consistent with one another?
- [ ] Held eye contact; avoided reading?
- [ ] Recovered after interruptions?
- [ ] Did the FOH script sound human?

### Audience instructions
Tell audience teams at 44:00: *"While others pitch, you're advisors. Write down one thing they did that you'd steal and one question you'd have asked."* Audience members **do not** speak during pitches.

## 6. The Debrief (70:00 – 80:00)

### 70:00 | Break character
Stand up. Thank the Role Player and have them step out of persona, ideally with a visible gesture (a handshake or a deep breath).

> "Okay, we're out of the room. That was a simulation, and the stress you felt was real. Let's talk about why."

Quickly reveal your own observations for 60 seconds. Name two specific, positive things you saw. Do not rank teams unless you promised to.

### Debrief Framework (use 3 to 4 questions, about 2 minutes each)

**Q1. Decision-making with incomplete data**
> "You never knew whether the journalist was right. How did you decide anyway? What would you have wanted to know, and what would it have cost you to find out before 5:00?"

*Connect to:* Production incidents, security breaches, data leaks, outages. Leaders almost never have full information when it matters. The skill is making a defensible, reversible-where-possible decision, then updating as new facts arrive.

*Listen for:* explicit assumptions, risk ranking, and willingness to say "we don't know."

**Q2. Owning bad news**
> "Who had to tell someone something they didn't want to hear? How did you decide how and when to say it, and what would have happened if you waited?"

*Connect to:* Disclosing a vulnerability, a missed deadline, a failed launch or a vendor failure. Bad news does not improve with age, and people trust leaders who bring it early.

*Listen for:* the contrast between "get ahead of the story" and "wait and see."

**Q3. The inject: adapting when the plan breaks**
> "At 35 minutes, everything changed. What did your team do in the first 60 seconds? Did you adjust your plan, defend your plan or freeze?"

*Connect to:* Vendor and third-party risk, legal exposure, contracts and dependencies that constrain your options. Ask: "Where does your own organization depend on a supplier whose claims you've never verified?" (For tech audiences: software dependencies, cloud vendors, data providers.)

**Q4. Communicating one decision to three audiences**
> "You wrote for the Owner, the Chef and the guest. What changed about your language each time? What happened when your three deliverables didn't match?"

*Connect to:* Asynchronous communication. In real organizations, you rarely get a live room. Your written BLUF, your runbook and your customer statement must each stand alone and still tell the same story.

*Listen for:* consistency vs. contradiction between internal and external messages.

**Optional Q5 (if time allows): Pressure and presence**
> "The Owner interrupted you. How did that feel, and what did you do with it?"

*Connect to:* Executive presence, and the fact that senior leaders often interrupt not out of hostility but to test conviction.

### 78:00 | Close (90 seconds)
Give three takeaways, then end on time:
1. **Lead with the answer.** BLUF applies to emails, incident reports and status pages as much as to restaurants.
2. **Triage by recoverability.** Money comes back more often than trust.
3. **Own the news.** The story is going to be told either way. Choose whether you're the narrator.

> "In a real crisis, you won't have 25 minutes or a facilitator. You'll have the habits you practiced today. Thank you."

## Contingency Plans

| Problem | Response |
|---|---|
| Running behind during Primer | Cut the BLUF drill; keep the contrast demo. Never cut the pitch structure. |
| A team finishes early | Challenge them: "Your FOH script. Say it out loud to a stranger. Does it hold up?" |
| A team is stuck | Ask only: "What's the first thing you'd say to the Owner?" |
| Pitches run long | Chime at 3:00 and enforce the hard stop at about 6:00 per team. |
| Role Player goes too harsh | Use the agreed signal; give the team a 15-second reset ("Take a breath, Owner, give them one more shot."). |
| Fewer or more than 20 participants | Use 3 or 5 teams. Keep 25 minutes of War Room, shorten Q&A to 2 minutes if 5 teams. |
| Technology failure (timer) | Use a phone countdown on a document camera, or a big analog timer. |
| Strong emotional response | Pause briefly and acknowledge it; it's evidence the simulation works. Return to structure. |
